And then there's the signing.

CBS Sports reporter Matt Zenitz reported on November 23rd that the Vikings are bringing Tristan Leigh back to the practice squad.

Leigh is a familiar name around TCO Performance Center. He's been with Minnesota earlier this season, and the team has released and re-signed him several times.

So who is Tristan Leigh?

He's a rookie. Six-foot-four, 312 pounds.

He came out of high school as a five-star recruit. Then he became Clemson's starting left tackle from 2023 through 2025.

That's three full seasons starting at left tackle for one of the top programs in college football. He's protected quarterbacks against SEC and ACC pass rushers. That experience matters.

Now, let's be clear about what this move is, and what it isn't.

Adding Leigh does not mean Minnesota has named a new starter. It's a depth move. It gives the team another developmental option while it figures out the Darrisaw situation.

And there's a roster puzzle attached.

The practice squad is already at its 16-player limit. So to bring Leigh back, Minnesota has to either release someone, or promote a player to the 53-man roster. And a promotion would require a corresponding move on the active roster too.

The other offensive linemen on the practice squad right now are Henry Byrd, Jack Nelson, and Josh Priebe.

So keep an eye on the transaction wire this week. Somebody is going to be affected by this move.

So what does Leigh actually bring to the table?

The scouting reports show both the upside and the work still ahead.

NFL.com's Lance Zierlein described him as a talented but inconsistent prospect. He raised concerns about Leigh's ability to handle power, especially in the running game.

And that's worth noting, because the Vikings just ran for only 69 yards on 21 carries against San Francisco. O'Connell said himself that the run game wasn't good enough.

But a pre-draft evaluation from Pro Football Network was a lot more optimistic about his ceiling.

It argued that Leigh offered unusual explosiveness, raw power, physicality, and a rare combination of length and width. And it expected that teams could get him outside the first round.

It highlighted his energetic movement and quick twitch. And it pointed to flashes of forceful hands and core strength in pass protection.

His anchor, meaning his ability to hold his ground against a bull rush.

His footwork.

The precision and timing of his hands.

And the coordination between his upper and lower body when he's protecting the quarterback.

The same report called him a people mover in the run game, and saw upside in both phases.

And here's an interesting part. It suggested that his explosive power in tight spaces might translate to guard, if tackle doesn't turn out to be his best position.

And speaking of Nick Bosa, let's talk about that hit on Cousins that could still bring punishment from the league.

It happened in the first quarter. The Vikings faced second-and-7 at the San Francisco 22-yard line.

Cousins threw incomplete toward T.J. Hockenson. But Bosa was flagged for roughing the passer. That gave Minnesota 15 yards and a first down.

Bosa looked like he tried to brace himself as he fell. But he still landed with his body weight on Cousins.

And that brought back the familiar argument. What is this rule really supposed to prevent? And how is a defender supposed to follow it while finishing a tackle?

A fine is possible. But nothing has been announced yet.

The listed amount for a first offense is $17,911. A second offense carries $23,882.

And remember, a flag on the field and a fine from the league are two separate decisions. The NFL can also review plays that weren't flagged at all.

This was the only flagged penalty in the game involving a finable offense. No Vikings player was flagged for one. But that doesn't guarantee the league won't find something in its review.

The body-weight rule has been controversial since 2018. Enforcement increased so much that the backlash forced the league to release videos clarifying what it wanted from defenders.

Roughing-the-passer calls climbed above half a call per game after that change. In 2021, the rate was 0.54 per game.

This season, it's above 0.44, the highest rate since 2021.

Through Week 10, the league had issued 21 fines classified as hits on quarterbacks or roughing the passer. Four involved body weight.

And the fines haven't been consistent. Baltimore's Trey Hendrickson and a Washington defender each got $17,911. New Orleans linebacker Danny Stutsman got a much smaller fine under the same category. And Bosa's own teammate, Keion White, was fined $13,770 for a roughing and body-weight violation after Week 2.

But here's the important part. This debate shouldn't hide what actually decided the game.
