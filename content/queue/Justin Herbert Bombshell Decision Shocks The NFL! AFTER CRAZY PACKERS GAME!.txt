Justin Herbert Bombshell Shocks The NFL! Did The Chargers LIE About The Vikings’ Call?

Vikings fans, before Minnesota landed Kirk Cousins at the trade deadline, it made a phone call that could still change the future of this franchise.

The Vikings called the Los Angeles Chargers about Justin Herbert.

And now there’s a controversy. Because the Chargers’ general manager went to a podium this week and said, essentially, that nobody ever called.

So who’s telling the truth? What really happened between Minnesota and Los Angeles? And is a Justin Herbert trade to the Vikings still possible, just a few months from now?

Welcome back to Vikings Floor. This one is a big deal for the future of the quarterback position in Minnesota. Let’s get into it.

Let’s start with what was said, because the timeline tells the story.

On Thursday, Chargers general manager Joe Hortiz stood in front of reporters and said that no one ever called about Justin Herbert.

No one ever called.

But two days before he said that, Jordan Schultz reported that the Minnesota Vikings called the Chargers about Herbert, and that the Chargers shut it down right away.

And five days before Hortiz’s comment, Adam Schefter and Ian Rapoport reported that rival general managers who called about Herbert were getting a hard no.

So either three of the most plugged-in reporters in football all got the same thing wrong, or the Chargers’ general manager said something on camera that doesn’t line up with the reporting.

Let’s walk through the full timeline, because it matters.

On October 13th, Adam Schefter said a source with the Chargers told him the idea of trading Justin Herbert had never once been uttered inside the organization, and never would be.

Fine. That’s a clear statement.

Then on November 7th, Schefter and Rapoport reported that general managers around the league had called about Herbert, and about wide receiver Ladd McConkey, and the Chargers told every one of them no.

Then on deadline day, Jordan Schultz reported that the Vikings asked about Justin Herbert, Daniel Jones, and Geno Smith before they landed on Kirk Cousins.

And Schultz’s report said the Chargers immediately shut down all inquiries about Herbert.

So Minnesota ended up paying a third-round pick for Cousins instead.

Then two days after that, Hortiz said no one ever called on Justin Herbert.

Now, to be fair to Hortiz, maybe what he meant is that nobody made a real offer.

A team calling to check the temperature and a team putting three first-round picks on the table are two completely different things. And no reporter has said there was an actual offer.

But if that’s what he meant, he could have said that. Instead, he said nobody called.

And two separate reports, from three different reporters, say the phone rang.

So the most likely version of events is this: Minnesota called. The Chargers hung up. And then the Chargers acted like the call never happened.

Now let’s talk about why the Vikings would even make that call, and why it makes so much sense.

Because the Chargers are in freefall.

They’re 0-8. The only team in the NFL without a win.

They’ve lost 11 straight games going back to last season, if you count the playoff loss. That ties the longest losing streak in franchise history.

And the offense has been almost impossible to watch.

The Chargers went 37 straight drives without scoring a touchdown. That’s 12 quarters. Three full games of football.

When they finally scored one on Sunday, they were already down 38-9 to the Houston Texans.

Justin Herbert is tied with Drake Maye for the most interceptions in the NFL, with 11.

He was sacked eight times in that Houston game, a career high. He’s been sacked 31 times in eight games, the second-most in football.

And the fans have noticed. The LA Times reported that get-in prices on the resale market dipped under $15, and SoFi Stadium was about half full on Sunday.

So you can see why teams called. A franchise quarterback on a winless team. That’s exactly when you check in.

But here’s the question that matters most for Vikings fans.

Is Justin Herbert the problem in Los Angeles? Or is it everything around him?

Because if Minnesota is thinking about Herbert as its long-term answer, that question decides everything.

And there’s a strong case that it isn’t Herbert.

You don’t put together the seasons Justin Herbert has put together and then suddenly forget how to play football at 28, in what should be the prime of your career.

Look at what’s happening around him.

He’s been sacked 31 times in eight games.

His best receiver, Ladd McConkey, has been in and out of the lineup all season, and didn’t play a snap against Houston because of back spasms.

And he’s playing in Mike McDaniel’s offense.

ESPN’s Jeremy Fowler wrote earlier this season that Herbert has been at his best as a true pocket passer, and that McDaniel’s offense in Miami was built on throwing with anticipation to a spot.

Even Kurt Warner went on The Dan Patrick Show and put most of the blame on the scheme.

Now here’s the part that makes Vikings fans pay attention.

Remember what ESPN’s Aaron Schatz argued a couple of weeks ago? He asked whether Kevin O’Connell could bring out the version of Herbert that looked so dangerous early in his career. Something McDaniel hasn’t been able to do.

O’Connell’s offense is built around timing, structure, and a quarterback who can work from the pocket. That’s exactly the kind of system where Herbert has thrived before.

But there’s one stat that cuts the other way, and we have to be honest about it.

Nine of Herbert’s 11 interceptions came when he wasn’t under pressure.

Nine out of 11. In a clean pocket.

That’s a real concern. Interceptions under pressure, you can blame on the line. Interceptions from a clean pocket fall on the quarterback.

But there’s context here too. When a quarterback has been hit as much as Herbert has, in a system where he’s still learning where everyone is supposed to be, the clean pockets stop feeling clean. A quarterback who’s been sacked 31 times starts feeling pressure even when it isn’t there. He speeds up his process. He forces throws.

Again, you don’t forget how to play football at 28.

So if it’s everything around him, why would the Chargers ever trade him?

Because fixing everything around him costs years, and it costs picks. And the bill for Justin Herbert is about to get huge.

Look at his cap hit. Herbert counts about $58 million against the cap next year, and about $71 million the year after.

To put that in context, Over the Cap projects that $71 million at roughly 20 percent of the entire salary cap going to one player.

And back in October, Schefter said a high-level executive told him Herbert could be worth something like three first-round picks.

To be clear, that’s a hypothetical. Nobody has offered three first-round picks for Herbert.

But it gives you an idea of the price. And remember the hypothetical starting point from ESPN’s Aaron Schatz piece: three first-round picks, with Jordan Addison or Dallas Turner as examples of young players who could be part of the discussion.

That’s an enormous price for Minnesota.

And there’s more that complicates any Herbert-to-Minnesota idea.

First, Herbert has a no-trade clause. He gets to pick where he goes. So any team has to convince him first.

Second, the Vikings are 6-2. That means their first-round picks are going to be late ones. Three late first-round picks are worth a lot less than three high ones. The Chargers would almost certainly want more.

Third, the money. Over the Cap says that if the Chargers trade Herbert next spring before June 1st, they eat about $50 million in dead money and save less than $8 million. That’s a massive financial hit for Los Angeles.

So saying no to Minnesota in November? That’s actually the one decision in this whole situation that’s easy to defend.

The trade deadline was never the real window for a move like this.

The real window is the offseason, before the middle of March, when $25 million of Herbert’s 2028 salary becomes fully guaranteed.

That’s the date Vikings fans should circle.

Because think about Minnesota’s quarterback situation heading into 2027.

Kirk Cousins is 38. He’s here to win now. His contract has a $10 million commitment for 2027, and a 2028 salary of more than $78 million that becomes fully guaranteed on the fifth day of the next league year. The Vikings will have to address that one way or another.

Kyler Murray was demoted to backup. J.J. McCarthy is gone. Carson Wentz is likely QB3.

Minnesota’s 2027 quarterback situation is completely unsettled.

And that’s why the Herbert call matters so much. It tells you the Vikings are already thinking big about the future of the position. They weren’t just looking for a quick fix at the deadline. They were looking at franchise quarterbacks.

And the Chargers’ situation could look very different by March.

They’re on track for the No. 1 pick in the NFL draft. And the two biggest mock drafts, from PFF and FOX, both have them taking Jeremiah Smith, the wide receiver from Ohio State, who had 1,200 yards and 14 touchdowns through 10 weeks.

Think about what a Chargers rebuild could look like. Jeremiah Smith first overall. A rookie quarterback on a cheap deal. Draft picks spent on the offensive line. And whatever haul they get for Herbert to fund it all.

That’s a very real plan. And it could make Herbert available.

Now, if Herbert doesn’t happen, there’s another path for Minnesota: the draft.

And this year’s quarterback class looks deep.

Sports Illustrated’s mock back in September had seven quarterbacks going in the first round. CBS had five in early October. FOX had six last week.

But drafting a quarterback is never a sure thing, and this class shows it.

The CBS mock had Arch Manning falling out of the first round completely.

Oregon’s Dante Moore suffered a concussion in September and isn’t expected back this season.

And the first quarterback off the board in that FOX mock is Drew Mestemaker from Oklahoma State, a player most fans couldn’t have picked out of a lineup back in August.

So the depth is there. But it’s unpredictable. Which is exactly why a proven quarterback like Herbert would be so attractive.

And there’s one more Vikings connection in this story. Because the Chargers had other players Minnesota could have been interested in at the deadline too.

Let’s talk about how the rest of the Chargers’ deadline played out, because it’s frustrating to watch from the outside.

Hortiz said he’d been on the phone with teams since Week 3. He said the Chargers were open to moving players for the right package. And he said nobody put together anything that blew them away.

But five days earlier, Schefter and Rapoport reported that the Chargers were telling teams they had no interest in trading Khalil Mack, that they didn’t like the message of giving up on the season, and that even though they were winless, they weren’t acting like it in any trade conversation.

Then on the morning of the deadline, Jordan Schultz reported that the Chargers had become more receptive to trading players over the previous 24 hours.

So which was it? Working the phones for six weeks, or telling everyone no for six weeks and then cracking the door open at the last possible moment?

And look at what happened around the league.

On deadline day, CBS counted 18 trades across the NFL this season.

The Eagles got a first- and a second-round pick for Jordan Davis.

The Titans got a second-round pick for Amani Hooker and a fifth.

The Raiders got a third-round pick for Kirk Cousins, the deal Minnesota made.

The Colts got a fourth and a fifth for Josh Downs and a seventh.

And the Chargers, the only winless team in the league, finished the deadline with zero completed trades. They even agreed to one deal involving one of their team captains, and it fell apart late on a Wednesday night.

Remember Keenan Allen? The Chargers let him sit unsigned until the middle of August. The Colts picked him up, and at the deadline flipped him to the 49ers for a sixth-round pick. So a 34-year-old receiver the Chargers didn’t want had trade value. And the Chargers are saying their own veterans didn’t.

To be fair, The Athletic reported that the Chargers’ veterans on expiring deals, including Dalvin Tomlinson, didn’t draw much interest.

And remember Dalvin Tomlinson? He was one of the names connected to the Vikings a few weeks ago as a possible reunion on the defensive line. He spent 2021 and 2022 in Minnesota. He’s still in Los Angeles.

And then there’s Khalil Mack.

Mack is a sure-fire Hall of Famer. He’s 35, with 116 career sacks. He came back to the Chargers this year on a one-year, $18 million fully guaranteed deal.

He has three sacks in eight games, on pace for about six. That’s a big drop from the 17 sacks he had just three years ago.

He’s still a very good player. And he’s exactly the kind of veteran a contender calls about in November.

Remember, Mack’s name came up in the Vikings’ conversation too, as a possible mentor for Dallas Turner and a way to keep him away from Chicago.

But to his credit, Mack has never asked out publicly. He told The Athletic that the team knows how he feels, and that he wants to be there and help them win. Jim Harbaugh called him one of the pillars of the team.

So Mack stays. Tomlinson stays. Herbert stays. At least for now.

So let’s put it all together.

The Vikings called the Chargers about Justin Herbert before the deadline, according to Jordan Schultz. The Chargers shut it down immediately. Minnesota pivoted to Kirk Cousins for a third-round pick.

Chargers general manager Joe Hortiz later said no one ever called about Herbert, which doesn’t line up with reporting from Schultz, Schefter, and Rapoport.

Herbert is struggling in Los Angeles, with 11 interceptions and 31 sacks in eight games. But there’s a strong case that the scheme and the offensive line are the bigger problems, and that a pocket-friendly system like O’Connell’s could bring out his best.

The price would be enormous: possibly three first-round picks, a no-trade clause, and a cap hit rising to $71 million.

And the real window isn’t now. It’s this offseason, before mid-March, when $25 million of Herbert’s 2028 salary becomes fully guaranteed.

With Cousins on a short-term deal and Minnesota’s 2027 quarterback situation wide open, that’s a window Vikings fans should watch very closely.

But first, the present. Kirk Cousins and the Vikings face the Packers at Lambeau today. And what happens over the next eight games will go a long way toward deciding how aggressive Minnesota needs to be this offseason.
