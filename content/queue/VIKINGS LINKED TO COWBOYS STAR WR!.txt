VIKINGS LINKED TO COWBOYS STAR WR!

Vikings fans, the pressure to fix this offense is building.

A trade proposal links Minnesota to a star receiver from Dallas. After a major shakeup in Arizona, another quarterback has emerged as a possible target. And offensive coordinator Wes Phillips faced tough questions this week, and his answer only added to the tension.

Buffalo is coming to town on Monday Night Football. The trade deadline is the very next day. And Minnesota has some big decisions to make.

Welcome back to Vikings Floor. Let’s break it all down.

The latest proposed fix for Minnesota’s offense isn’t a running back. It isn’t a quarterback.

It’s Dallas Cowboys receiver George Pickens.

On Monday, CBS Sports writer Garrett Podell named the Vikings as one of five logical trade deadline destinations for the 25-year-old wideout.

And his reasoning starts with a contrast that should bother every Vikings fan.

Minnesota is 6-1. The Vikings hold the No. 2 seed in the NFC.

And yet, the offense is averaging just 20 points per game, the sixth-lowest figure in the league.

The passing attack is producing only 165.6 yards per game. That’s the second-fewest in the entire NFL.

Read that again. Second-fewest passing yards in the league.

On a team with Justin Jefferson, the 2022 Offensive Player of the Year. And Jordan Addison, a former first-round pick.

That’s the contradiction at the heart of this season. Elite weapons. Bottom-of-the-league passing production.

Podell’s argument is that another weapon could help Kyler Murray find his rhythm.

And even with Minnesota already showing up in Jonathan Taylor rumors, Pickens brings production that’s worth talking about.

Let’s look at what he did last season.

George Pickens earned second-team All-Pro honors and his first Pro Bowl selection. He caught 93 passes for 1,429 yards and nine touchdowns.

Fourteen hundred twenty-nine yards. That’s a No. 1 receiver’s season.

He had shown that potential during his first three seasons in Pittsburgh, where quarterback play and play-calling were seen as possible limitations. In Dallas, he finally broke out.

But now his situation has gotten unsettled.

The Cowboys are 3-5 after a 33-30 overtime loss to Arizona. And Pickens finished that game with just two catches on four targets.

Two catches. In an overtime game his team lost.

And then video showed him running into the locker room alone, just seconds after the loss. That moment led Podell to encourage Dallas to consider moving him.

A frustrated star on a 3-5 team. That’s how trade conversations start.

There’s also a financial decision attached to any deal.

Pickens is playing on the franchise tag. He’ll become a free agent in March. And Spotrac projects his market value at four years and $138 million.

To be clear, that’s a contract projection, not a reported trade price.

So Minnesota would have options.

The Vikings could treat him as a rental for the second half of 2026. They could pursue a longer commitment. Or they could use a franchise tag on him again next year, then explore another trade.

That last approach could help recover some of the draft capital spent to get him. But it would depend on a future market, and it wouldn’t make the original trade free.

But the bigger question is whether another receiver actually addresses the real problem.

And this is where the proposal gets complicated.

Murray is already struggling to consistently involve Jefferson and Addison, whose production has fallen short of their usual standard.

Remember the Detroit game. Jefferson got nine targets, caught five, and gained just 33 yards. His longest catch went for nine yards.

So if the quarterback is struggling to get the ball to the receivers he already has, would adding a third star receiver fix that? Or would it just spread the same limited production across more players?

There are also concerns about Pickens himself, specifically how he handles adversity when his role is limited.

Think about what that could mean in Minnesota.

Jefferson has already shown frustration this season. He didn’t speak to reporters after the Detroit loss. Now add another receiver who expects targets, and who reacted the way he did after a two-catch game in Dallas.

That could create more frustration in the receiver room, reduce Jefferson’s opportunities even further, and use up assets Minnesota might otherwise spend on a running back.

And look at where the real imbalance is.

Minnesota’s defense ranks first in points allowed and second in yards allowed. The offense sits in the bottom six in both scoring and total yards.

So the need on offense is obvious. But whether a receiver is the right fix is a different question.

To be clear, this is an outside proposal and a long-shot possibility. There’s no reported agreement, and no confirmed Vikings pursuit.

That difference between an appealing proposal and an actual front office plan matters even more in the next story.

Because Alec Lewis of The Athletic has offered a more restrained picture of Minnesota’s approach.

Lewis described the decision facing Nolan Teasley and Vikings leadership as a balance between patience and aggression. And he said the outcome will tell us a lot about how this front office values team-building and resource allocation.

His reporting suggests the more likely path is to supplement the team’s existing strengths, without compromising its ability to build through the draft.

That doesn’t mean Minnesota has ruled out a trade.

It means that fans expecting a dramatic, blockbuster deal should recognize that keeping future flexibility appears to be part of the calculation.

And that lines up with what Kevin O’Connell said a couple of weeks ago, that he wants Teasley to have the resources to put his own stamp on the roster in the next draft.

So far, there’s been no major Vikings trade.

Despite all the speculation about Jonathan Taylor, Maxx Crosby, Kirk Cousins, Daniel Jones, and Justin Herbert, nothing has happened yet.

And those names represent very different ways of improving the roster. A star running back. An elite pass rusher. Two veteran quarterbacks. A franchise quarterback. Showing up in the rumor cycle doesn’t mean Minnesota is negotiating for all of them.

And here’s the timing that makes this week so dramatic.

Before the deadline arrives, the Vikings host the Buffalo Bills on Monday Night Football in Minneapolis.

Monday night. Then the deadline is Tuesday afternoon.

So depending on that result, the front office will be making its final decisions with a record of either 7-1 or 6-2.

That’s a big difference. At 7-1, you’re back on top of the conference conversation, and patience looks smart. At 6-2, with back-to-back losses and the offense still stuck, the pressure to make a move goes way up.

The question will be whether to spend aggressively at running back or quarterback during the season, or save resources for an offseason when some significant players could leave.

And the first loss has already changed how some people see this team.

Bleacher Report dropped Minnesota from second to sixth in its power rankings after the 19-15 loss at Detroit.

Maurice Moton argued that the Vikings are still one of the league’s best teams, despite mediocre quarterback play. But he warned that their position could slip during the upcoming stretch.

And look at that stretch. Buffalo. Then Green Bay at Lambeau Field. Then San Francisco.

That’s a brutal three-game run. A dangerous Bills team. A Packers team with a healthier Micah Parsons, on the road at Lambeau. And a 49ers team that just added DeForest Buckner.

Moton’s concern is whether Murray can generate enough offense to keep pace.

And he pointed out something interesting. Carson Wentz came into the season-opening win over Green Bay and threw three touchdown passes. Murray has five touchdown passes across six starts.

Three touchdowns in one game for Wentz. Five in six starts for Murray.

That comparison is part of Moton’s argument. It’s not evidence that the coaching staff has decided to switch quarterbacks. But it explains why the Wentz conversation keeps coming up.

Still, these next three opponents give Minnesota a demanding chance to answer the doubters. The defense has set the standard. Now the offense has to meet it.

Now let’s talk about a new quarterback option, and it came out of the same Arizona game that intensified the Pickens discussion.

During the Cowboys game, the Cardinals benched Jacoby Brissett. Gardner Minshew II came in and led Arizona’s comeback win.

Then on Monday, Arizona announced that Minshew would start in Week 9, with rookie Carson Beck as his backup.

That pushed Brissett, who had started the first eight games, all the way down to third on the depth chart.

From starter to third string in one week.

Pro Football Talk’s Mike Florio read that move as a message to the rest of the league: Arizona would entertain offers before the deadline.

To be clear, that’s Florio’s interpretation, not a quoted announcement from the Cardinals saying Brissett is available.

But Florio discussed the veteran as a possible addition for a contender looking for quarterback depth. And he left open the possibility of Minnesota even considering him as a replacement for Murray.

Separately, Trevor Ripley of Vikings On SI included Brissett among four realistic quarterback trade targets on Monday.

Ripley suggested that a late day-three pick could potentially be enough to get him.

But he also explained why a cheap price wouldn’t automatically make the move worth it.

Let’s look at the numbers.

Brissett has four games this season with at least 270 passing yards. That’s real production.

But his overall performance has dropped off from last year.

Across eight starts this season, he completed 63.3 percent of his passes, with 10 touchdowns and seven interceptions, averaging 5.6 yards per attempt.

His record as a starter was 3-5, and that includes the Dallas win, which was credited to him because he started the game.

Last season, under a different offensive coordinator, Brissett averaged 6.9 yards per attempt and threw 23 touchdowns against eight interceptions in 14 games.

But Arizona also led the league in passing attempts that season, and that volume likely helped his overall totals.

And he’s 33. He’s played for six teams over 11 seasons.

So Minnesota would be evaluating an experienced journeyman, not a long-term franchise answer.

Ripley’s other concern was how hard it would be to learn O’Connell’s offense quickly enough to make a real difference.

He argued the system hasn’t shown much adaptability, which makes a midseason transition a big challenge.

That’s the same warning we heard from The Athletic’s Ted Nguyen last week: midseason quarterback trades rarely work.

So why do all these quarterback alternatives keep coming up?

Look at Kyler Murray’s numbers.

Five passing touchdowns. Three interceptions. A 64.9 completion percentage. And 6.6 yards per attempt, across six starts.

To be fair, he’s won five of those six starts. The record is excellent.

But look at the team numbers.

Minnesota ranks 31st in passing yards and 24th in passing touchdowns.

And despite Murray’s mobility, the Vikings are 22nd in yards per carry, and dead last in rushing touchdowns.

All of that adds up to an offense ranked 27th in scoring and 29th in total yards.

Meanwhile, the defense has allowed the fewest points in the league and the second-fewest yards through eight weeks.

So the incentive to improve the quarterback production is obvious.

The unresolved question is whether someone like Brissett could actually provide that improvement after arriving in the middle of the season.

Then there’s the coaching staff’s public response, which added another layer this week.

On Tuesday, offensive coordinator Wes Phillips was asked about Murray’s performance against Detroit.

And his answer didn’t give a direct assessment of the quarterback’s play.

As shared by ESPN’s Kevin Seifert on X, Phillips said he didn’t want to offer one overarching explanation pointing to one specific problem.

He emphasized that Minnesota had lost a tough road game against a tough division opponent. He acknowledged the result hurt, and said the team has to get better.

Phillips also mentioned Tyler Williams, the team’s vice president of health and performance, and his staff, saying the Vikings would see where things stood at the position as the week went on.

And he finished by saying Minnesota would actively pursue every avenue available to get better.

Every avenue.

Now, those comments are worth separating carefully.

His reference to Tyler Williams is about the health evaluation, Murray’s hamstring. That’s the injury question.

His refusal to name one big problem leaves the performance question mostly unanswered.

And promising to pursue every avenue doesn’t, on its own, confirm a quarterback trade, a benching, or any other specific move.

Phillips wasn’t obligated to publicly blame Murray. Coordinators rarely do. But his answer also did little to clarify what the coaches believe has to change.

And that matters, because the choice isn’t just “keep everything the same” or “bring in someone from outside.”

Better quarterback execution and better coaching adjustments are still part of the solution, even if the roster stays exactly as it is.

Remember what O’Connell said after Detroit: shotgun runs, shotgun play-action, screens, movement plays, getting Murray on the perimeter. Those are coaching solutions, not trade solutions.

And Lewis’s reporting about protecting the draft-based approach applies here too. The staff’s desire to improve has to work alongside the front office’s decisions about cost and future needs.

With Buffalo coming before the deadline, Minnesota has one more game to find out whether its current personnel and approach can produce a stronger offensive performance.

So let’s put it all together.

Minnesota’s position is strong enough to justify ambition. Six and one. The No. 2 seed in the NFC. The best defense in football.

But these stories show why the answer can’t just be “go get the biggest available name.”

George Pickens would add receiving talent. A 1,429-yard, second-team All-Pro season is real. But Minnesota already can’t consistently feed Justin Jefferson and Jordan Addison, and there are questions about how Pickens handles a limited role.

Jacoby Brissett would add another quarterback option, for possibly a late day-three pick. But he’s a 33-year-old journeyman whose numbers have dropped this season, and learning O’Connell’s system midseason would be a big challenge.

Either move would come with questions about fit, cost, and immediate impact.

Alec Lewis’s reporting suggests the front office wants to help this team without sacrificing its ability to build through the draft.

And Wes Phillips’s comments leave the offensive evaluation wide open.

Buffalo on Monday Night Football is the next test. And the November 10th deadline follows less than 24 hours later.
