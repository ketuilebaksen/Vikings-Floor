What if I told you the Minnesota Vikings were one failed phone call away from a quarterback decision that could have completely changed the direction of their season?

And what if Kirk Cousins’s return wasn’t just a reunion with a familiar face, but a desperate attempt to keep one of the NFL’s most promising teams from watching its championship hopes collapse?

Because new information about Minnesota’s quarterback situation has revealed just how close this organization came to heading into its huge NFC North showdown with Green Bay with a completely different plan.

And once you understand what was happening behind the scenes, the decision to bring Cousins back looks even bigger.

Here’s the bombshell.

NFL insider Tom Pelissero delivered a fascinating revelation during an appearance on KFAN.

According to Pelissero, if the Vikings had failed to complete the trade with the Las Vegas Raiders for Kirk Cousins, Max Brosmer probably would have been the starting quarterback against Green Bay.

Max Brosmer.

Not Carson Wentz. Not a hobbled Kyler Murray. The third-string quarterback.

Think about that for a second.

With the season at a critical point, Minnesota was seriously facing the possibility of turning to Brosmer for one of its biggest division games of the year.

Not because the coaching staff had suddenly discovered an exciting new offensive formula. But because the alternatives had become so hard to trust.

At Lambeau Field. Against a red-hot Jordan Love. With the division on the line. And the starting quarterback would have been the guy who, just a few days ago, Kevin O’Connell said was still in the middle of his growth and development.

That’s how close this came.

And that’s where this story gets especially uncomfortable for Carson Wentz.

The veteran had a real opportunity to show he could keep Minnesota’s offense running while Kyler Murray dealt with his injury.

Instead, his performance against the Buffalo Bills raised even more concerns about whether the Vikings could afford to keep putting him under center.

Wentz completed just eight of his 25 passes, for 81 yards and two interceptions.

Those numbers are alarming enough on their own. But the deeper evaluation makes it even more troubling.

The performance included four turnover-worthy plays. And his adjusted completion percentage was just 42.9 percent.

Adjusted completion percentage accounts for drops, throwaways, and spikes. So even after giving Wentz credit for everything that wasn’t his fault, he was still completing fewer than half of his catchable throws.

For a team with real playoff ambitions, those numbers created a problem that couldn’t be ignored anymore.

And remember the other numbers from that night. Minnesota had just 178 total yards. Buffalo held the ball for more than 42 minutes. Only one of Wentz’s 11 drives lasted more than five plays.

Minnesota wasn’t just dealing with an inconsistent passing game. The organization was facing a situation where its quarterback play was threatening to undermine everything the rest of the roster had accomplished.

And with Green Bay waiting at Lambeau, there was almost no room for another offensive disaster.

Now here’s what makes this even more remarkable: the comparison to Max Brosmer’s own rough outing last season.

In a game against Seattle last year, Brosmer threw for about 150 yards, didn’t produce a touchdown, and was intercepted four times.

Four interceptions. A tough, tough day.

But the circumstances were very different.

He was facing a Seahawks defense that would eventually help carry Seattle to a Super Bowl championship. A rookie thrown in against one of the best defenses in football.

And here’s an interesting connection: Nolan Teasley was part of that Seattle championship front office. He knows exactly how good that defense was.

Wentz, on the other hand, went into the Buffalo game with preparation time and first-team reps. And the offense still struggled to generate even basic production.

And when Brosmer came in to finish Monday’s game, he went 6 for 7 for 58 yards. Small sample, mop-up duty, but efficient.

That contrast helps explain why Minnesota’s quarterback situation had reached such a critical point.

If Murray couldn’t come back, and Cousins wasn’t available, turning to Brosmer wasn’t an exciting solution.

It was just starting to look like the more reasonable option between two uncomfortable choices.

But here’s the part of this story that deserves even more attention.

What would another disappointing quarterback decision have meant for the Vikings’ locker room?

Think about what Minnesota’s defense has been contributing.

This unit has played at an exceptional level all season. It’s given the organization a foundation that keeps it competitive against tough opponents.

Held Detroit to 19 points. Held Buffalo to 23. Leads the league in making offenses play from behind the sticks, according to Matt LaFleur himself.

Now imagine being one of those defensive players.

Week after week, you execute your assignments, create opportunities, and fight to keep games within reach. And then you watch the offense repeatedly fail to capitalize.

Two straight losses where the defense did its job.

At some point, frustration becomes unavoidable.

To be clear, there’s no confirmed evidence of any kind of locker room revolt. But the possibility of growing frustration was a central concern raised in the discussion around this situation.

When players believe they have a real chance to compete for something meaningful, they expect the organization to do everything possible to support that chance.

And if the front office had answered that frustration by handing the ball to a third-string quarterback at Lambeau? That message could have been devastating.

That pressure doesn’t stop with the quarterbacks.

It also reaches head coach Kevin O’Connell, whose reputation has been built on quarterback development and offensive creativity.

O’Connell has built real credibility through his work with Minnesota’s offense. He helped Kirk Cousins to a 13-4 season in 2022. He revived Sam Darnold’s career.

But even an accomplished offensive coach faces hard questions when the most important position on the field becomes the team’s biggest weakness.

And those questions are getting louder. Remember, ESPN’s Dominique Foxworth said this week that if the Vikings miss the playoffs or flame out, they should fire O’Connell and promote Brian Flores. Jason McIntyre put O’Connell’s chances of being fired at 20 to 25 percent.

That’s why the Cousins trade matters beyond the transaction itself.

Minnesota officially reacquired the veteran from Las Vegas on November 10th, sending a 2027 third-round pick to the Raiders.

Cousins returned to the organization where he spent six seasons, from 2018 through 2023, and to a coaching system he already knows.

That familiarity gives Minnesota something it desperately needed: an experienced quarterback who understands the offense, and who has run it successfully before.

But the biggest question isn’t just whether Cousins can improve the offense.

It’s whether his arrival came at exactly the right moment to keep the Vikings from losing their chance to compete at the highest level.

One more loss, with Brosmer at quarterback, and this team could have dropped to 6-3. Three straight losses. In a division where every team has been at least 5-3.

Instead, Minnesota heads into Lambeau with a veteran who knows this offense, this receiver room, and this head coach.

And there’s another fascinating possibility tied to this trade deadline, involving a completely different offensive star Minnesota might have considered if the Cousins deal had fallen apart.

Now let’s look at one of the most intriguing questions about Minnesota’s deadline.

What if the Vikings hadn’t been able to get Kirk Cousins?

Could they have gone in a completely different direction on offense?

One possibility that was discussed: Colts running back Jonathan Taylor, a player whose arrival could have dramatically changed the structure of Minnesota’s offense.

The idea was based on the belief that Indianapolis would demand a first-round pick for Taylor. That would have been a big investment, possibly involving Minnesota’s 2027 first-round pick.

To be clear, this was speculation about an alternative scenario. It’s not confirmation that the Vikings had any agreement to acquire him.

And as it turned out, the Colts kept Taylor. They turned down multiple offers of second-round picks and held firm on their first-round asking price.

But the reasoning behind that possibility makes sense when you think about the organization’s urgency.

Minnesota wasn’t looking for a minor tweak. The Vikings needed meaningful offensive improvement. And their decisions showed a determination to take advantage of this roster’s potential, instead of letting another season slip away.

Think about how different those two paths would have been.

Path one: Cousins at quarterback, keep the first-round pick, rely on Jones and Mason in the backfield.

Path two: Brosmer at quarterback, Jonathan Taylor in the backfield, and a first-round pick gone.

The Vikings chose path one. And with a third-round price instead of a first, they kept their premium draft capital for the future.

But the Cousins trade also creates a fascinating dilemma.

Minnesota has fixed its immediate quarterback problem. But the long-term future of the position is still wide open.

Cousins is 38 years old. And while his familiarity with O’Connell’s system could help the Vikings right away, nobody can reasonably assume he’s the franchise’s long-term answer.

His contract creates uncertainty about 2027 too.

Remember the details. Minnesota inherits a $10 million commitment for 2027. And there’s a 2028 salary of more than $78 million that becomes fully guaranteed on the fifth day of the next league year.

Keeping him under his current deal would get extremely expensive. So a restructure is an important consideration if both sides want to keep this partnership going.

And that’s where next year’s NFL Draft gets especially interesting.

Several college quarterbacks have been mentioned as potential options for Minnesota.

Arch Manning. Dante Moore. Trinidad Chambliss. Austin Simmons. And LaNorris Sellers.

Each one has a different developmental profile. And there’s real uncertainty about where they’ll be drafted, or whether all of them will even declare.

But one possible strategy stands out.

Minnesota could draft a quarterback late in the first round, and let Cousins stay the starter.

Instead of throwing another young prospect into a demanding situation right away, the Vikings could give that player time to develop behind a veteran who knows the offense inside and out.

And that’s exactly what Las Vegas was doing with Fernando Mendoza. The Raiders signed Cousins as a bridge, a mentor for their rookie. Now Minnesota could use the same blueprint.

That approach could connect Minnesota’s immediate championship ambitions with its future at the most important position in the sport.

Think about how much smoother that would be than what happened with J.J. McCarthy. Drafted 10th overall, thrown into a tough situation, and traded to the Giants for a fifth-round pick. Now he’s 2-1 as New York’s starter.

A rookie who gets a year behind Kirk Cousins, learning the offense without the pressure of being the starter, might have a much better chance to succeed.

And there’s a human side to Carson Wentz’s situation too.

His struggles shouldn’t erase the physical punishment he’s taken, or the challenges he’s faced throughout his career.

From his promising 2017 season in Philadelphia, when he was an MVP candidate before a knee injury, to a difficult journey through multiple organizations, Wentz has lived through some of football’s harshest realities.

And remember, he’s a Minnesota guy at heart. He grew up rooting for the Vikings. Coming here was a homecoming for him too. Monday night didn’t go the way anyone wanted, least of all him.

But professional football ultimately demands hard decisions, especially when an organization believes its championship window is still open.

And that’s what makes this whole situation so compelling.

The Vikings brought back Kirk Cousins to solve an immediate problem.

But they still have to answer a much bigger question.

Who leads this franchise when Cousins is no longer the answer?

According to Tom Pelissero, if the Cousins trade had fallen through, Max Brosmer probably would have started at Lambeau.

Cousins solves the immediate problem for a 2027 third-round pick. But at 38, with a complicated contract, he isn’t the long-term answer.

But first, there’s Lambeau. And Kirk Cousins gets the chance to show this trade was worth it.
