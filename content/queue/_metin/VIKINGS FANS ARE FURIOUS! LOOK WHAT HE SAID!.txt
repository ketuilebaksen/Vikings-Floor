VIKINGS FANS ARE FURIOUS! LOOK WHAT HE SAID!

Vikings fans, you have to hear this, because things are getting personal.

Just when this defense was finally getting the respect it deserves, somebody decided to open his mouth and throw some serious shade at our guys.

And what was said is going to fire up a whole lot of people in Minnesota.

This is a defense that has been making opponents miserable all season. Eight sacks at Lambeau. Four takeaways. Fourteen points a game. Compared to the 2002 Buccaneers and the 2008 Steelers.

And somehow, there are still people acting like these guys aren’t the real deal.

But here’s the crazy part. Those comments might actually be the best thing that could have happened to this team right now.

And once you hear the full story, you’ll understand exactly why.

Welcome back to Vikings Floor. Let’s get into it.

What if I told you that one of the biggest advantages the Minnesota Vikings could have heading into Sunday’s showdown with the San Francisco 49ers didn’t come from a brilliant Brian Flores adjustment?

Not from a spectacular Dallas Turner performance. Not even from something that happened on the football field.

What if it came from a former Vikings player who just handed Minnesota’s defense a reason to take this matchup personally?

Because that’s exactly the controversy surrounding former NFL offensive lineman Alex Boone.

And what he said about this Vikings defense has sparked a debate that goes way beyond normal football criticism.

Here’s the interesting part. Boone didn’t just question Minnesota’s defensive strategy.

He essentially dismissed almost every major contributor on one of the NFL’s most impressive defenses, with one notable exception.

That exception was Dallas Turner.

But when you look at the numbers behind Andrew Van Ginkel, Jalen Redmond, and Levi Drake Rodriguez, his argument gets very hard to defend.

And the deeper you look, the more interesting this story gets, especially with San Francisco getting ready to face a defense that has been making life miserable for every quarterback it sees.

Let’s start with what Boone actually said.

The controversy began when Boone appeared in a conversation with San Francisco 49ers content creators and gave his assessment of Minnesota’s defensive personnel.

And there’s context worth knowing here. Boone spent most of his playing career with the 49ers before a stop in Minnesota. So he was talking to a San Francisco audience, about the defense the 49ers are about to face.

His central argument was that Dallas Turner was the one player opponents really need to worry about.

According to Boone, once you neutralize Turner, the rest of the defense is basically made up of players benefiting from the system around them.

Think about what that means.

Boone wasn’t just saying Brian Flores deserves a lot of credit for designing an effective defense. Everybody agrees with that.

He was implying that Minnesota’s other defensive playmakers don’t have the individual talent to earn real respect from opposing offenses.

And his comments about Andrew Van Ginkel made it even more controversial.

Boone questioned whether anyone should really be intimidated by Van Ginkel’s explosiveness or pass-rush moves.

Then he escalated it with an aggressive remark about grabbing Van Ginkel by his ponytail and driving him into the ground.

That comment immediately took the conversation from football analysis to something much more personal.

Criticizing a player’s technique is one thing. Questioning whether he can win individual matchups is completely legitimate. That’s what analysts do.

But using that kind of language about a player who’s been producing at an extremely high level raises a different question.

Is Boone actually evaluating Minnesota’s talent fairly? Or is he overlooking what these players have actually done?

Because when you look at the numbers, a fascinating pattern shows up.

Let’s start with the one player Boone did respect: Dallas Turner.

Turner came into the NFL with the reputation of a premium prospect. He played at Alabama, built an impressive college résumé, and became a first-round pick after Minnesota traded up to get him.

That background naturally creates expectations. When a highly regarded prospect starts breaking out, the recognition comes fast.

And Turner has broken out. He’s been tied for third in the NFL in sacks this season, and he got to Jordan Love at Lambeau.

So nobody’s arguing about Turner. He’s a star.

But Andrew Van Ginkel took a completely different path.

Van Ginkel came into the league as a fifth-round pick out of Wisconsin.

He didn’t arrive with Turner’s draft status. Not the same attention. Not the same expectations.

And yet, according to the Next Gen Stats figures cited in the discussion, Van Ginkel ranked sixth in the entire NFL in defensive get-off.

Get-off measures how quickly a defender explodes off the line of scrimmage after the snap. It’s one of the purest measures of a pass rusher’s burst.

And Van Ginkel ranked ahead of Turner, who was also in the top 10.

Read that again.

The player Boone said nobody should be intimidated by is one of the most explosive pass rushers off the snap in the entire league. Ahead of the one player Boone did respect.

That directly challenges Boone’s argument. If Van Ginkel has one of the quickest reactions off the line in football, dismissing his explosiveness is very hard to justify.

And Van Ginkel got Jordan Love on the ground at Lambeau too. He was one of the seven different Vikings who recorded a sack in that game.

Now let’s talk about Jalen Redmond.

Redmond’s journey is one of the best stories on this roster.

He went undrafted. He spent time in the XFL. And then he fought his way into Minnesota and established himself.

Redmond is exactly the kind of player who gets underestimated, because his path didn’t follow the traditional route of a celebrated prospect.

But the numbers paint an extraordinary picture of his impact.

Redmond carries an 83.7 overall Pro Football Focus grade. That ranks fourth among all defensive tackles in the NFL.

Fourth. Among every defensive tackle in the league.

His run defense grade is an outstanding 90.0. In the cited evaluation, that ranks first at his position.

First in the entire NFL against the run.

His pass-rushing grade is 61.8, which ranks 72nd.

And that distinction matters, because it shows exactly where his biggest contribution comes from.

Redmond isn’t being presented as an unstoppable pass rusher. His real value is in controlling the run game. Plugging gaps. Blowing up rushing lanes. Making life miserable for offensive linemen.

And think about who Minnesota is about to face.

Christian McCaffrey. One of the best running backs of this generation.

If there’s one Vikings defender whose job matters most against McCaffrey, it’s the player with the No. 1 run defense grade at his position. That’s Jalen Redmond.

So when Boone dismisses him as just a system player, he’s dismissing the guy whose skill set matches up most directly against San Francisco’s biggest weapon.

And Redmond still found ways to get to the quarterback at Lambeau. He was credited with a sack, and he was one of the team leaders with five pressures.

Now add Levi Drake Rodriguez to the conversation.

Rodriguez was a seventh-round pick. Another player whose road to the NFL offered almost no guarantee he’d become an important contributor.

Remember earlier this season, when a report said he had exceeded expectations in his first season as a starter?

Well, against the Green Bay Packers, he gave an emphatic answer to anyone questioning whether this defense’s success depends entirely on its coordinator.

Rodriguez posted a staggering 93.3 overall PFF grade. A 92.4 pass-rushing grade. Eight quarterback pressures. Two sacks. And two defensive stops.

A 93.3 overall grade. That’s elite. That’s not a system player having a good day. That’s a player dominating his matchup.

Those are not numbers you can casually dismiss as just the product of an effective scheme.

So let’s look at the full picture.

A first-round pick who’s tied for third in the league in sacks.

A fifth-round pick who’s sixth in the NFL in get-off.

An undrafted XFL player who’s the No. 1 run defender at his position.

A seventh-round pick who just posted a 93.3 grade with two sacks at Lambeau.

That’s not a defense full of system players. That’s a defense full of players who were overlooked, and who are now proving everyone wrong.

And that’s exactly why Boone’s comments could backfire.

Because this whole defense is built on chips on shoulders.

Van Ginkel, the fifth-rounder. Redmond, undrafted, from the XFL. Rodriguez, the seventh-rounder.

These are players who’ve heard “you’re not good enough” their entire careers. And now they’re hearing it again, from a former Viking, right before the biggest game of the season.

You think that’s going to make them less motivated? Or more?

That’s bulletin-board material. That’s the kind of quote that gets printed out and taped up in the locker room.

And it brings us to the central question behind this whole controversy.

How much of Minnesota’s success belongs to Brian Flores? And how much credit should go to the players executing his defense?

Let’s be fair here. Flores absolutely deserves recognition for what he’s built.

He’s blitzing at one of the highest rates in the league. He disguises coverages until the last second. He loops rushers from every angle. He sent extra rushers on 25 of Jordan Love’s 37 dropbacks at Lambeau.

That’s coaching. That’s scheme. That’s genius.

But here’s the thing. The defensive stats cited in the discussion show this unit allowing only 14 points per game. That’s generated comparisons to some of the most dominant defenses of the modern NFL era.

Remember the stat from Danny Parkins: allowing 14 or fewer points and fewer than 175 passing yards per game. Since 2000, only the 2002 Buccaneers and the 2008 Steelers have done that. Both won the Super Bowl.

A defense doesn’t reach that level just because someone draws up clever plays.

It takes defenders who can recognize assignments. Execute under pressure. Win physical battles. And perform together with remarkable consistency.

A great scheme with average players gives you a good defense. A great scheme with great players gives you a historic one.

And actually, there’s an interesting twist here.

Earlier this week, former players Chris Canty and Jeff Saturday compared this Vikings defense to the 2000s Pittsburgh Steelers. And Saturday said something that, on the surface, sounds a little like Boone.

He said those Steelers defenses didn’t have a bunch of players you were afraid of individually. But put them together, and they were a nightmare.

So in a way, both Saturday and Boone are pointing to the same thing: the power of the system.

But here’s the difference. Saturday meant it as a compliment. He was saying the whole is greater than the sum of its parts, and that the players are smart, disciplined, and play their roles perfectly.

Boone meant it as a dismissal. He was saying the parts aren’t very good.

And the numbers say Boone is wrong. Because when you look at the individual parts, they’re really good.

And that might be why Boone’s comments matter so much.

The argument isn’t just about whether Dallas Turner is Minnesota’s most talented defender anymore.

It’s about whether the rest of this defense has been denied the respect its performances have earned.

Van Ginkel. Redmond. Rodriguez. Kyle Van Noy. Eric Wilson. Elijah Williams. Blake Cashman. Harrison Smith. Josh Metellus. Byron Murphy Jr.

None of them were supposed to be stars. And together, they’ve become one of the most feared defenses in football.

But here’s what makes this even more interesting.

Boone also claimed he knows exactly how to beat Brian Flores’s defense.

And another San Francisco commentator has been criticizing Minnesota too.

So the bigger debate is this: have the 49ers genuinely identified a weakness in this defense?

Let’s think about it honestly.

If there is a way to attack this Vikings defense, it probably looks something like this.

First, the run game. When you blitz as much as Flores does, you commit extra players to the pass rush. That can leave running lanes open if a gap isn’t filled. And San Francisco has Christian McCaffrey.

But that’s exactly where Jalen Redmond’s No. 1 run defense grade comes in. That matchup might decide the game.

Second, quick passing. When pressure comes fast, you get the ball out fast. Screens. Quick slants. Getting the ball to playmakers like Deebo Samuel and George Kittle in space, before the rush gets home.

Third, the offensive line. Boone is a former offensive lineman, so this is where his perspective comes from. San Francisco has Trent Williams, one of the best left tackles in football. If the 49ers’ line can handle the pressure one-on-one, without needing extra blockers, then the blitz loses its advantage.

Those are real strategies. And Kyle Shanahan is one of the best offensive minds in football. He’ll have a plan.

But knowing how to beat a defense and actually doing it are two very different things.

Remember what Matt LaFleur said before the Green Bay game. He said Flores’s defense makes offenses play from behind the sticks more consistently than almost anything he’s seen. He said Green Bay needed a clean game.

Green Bay had a plan too. And it ended with eight sacks, four takeaways, and 10 points.

And remember what Jordan Love said before that game. He said that this time he had more film to study on Flores. But more film also means more looks to prepare for, and no guarantee which ones Flores will use.

That’s the problem with “knowing how to beat” Brian Flores. He doesn’t run the same defense twice.

So here’s my take.

Alex Boone is entitled to his opinion. He played in this league, he knows offensive line play, and his perspective has value.

But calling this defense one star and a bunch of system players ignores what the numbers clearly show.

And going after Andrew Van Ginkel the way he did? That was unnecessary.

The good news for Vikings fans? This defense now has a reason to play angry on Sunday night.

And when a defense like this plays angry, at altitude, in primetime, against the best offense in football?

That’s must-see television.

So let’s put it all together.

Former NFL lineman Alex Boone, speaking with 49ers content creators, said Dallas Turner is the only Vikings defender opponents need to worry about, and dismissed the rest as system players.

He questioned Andrew Van Ginkel’s explosiveness and made an aggressive personal comment about him.

But Van Ginkel ranks sixth in the NFL in defensive get-off, according to Next Gen Stats, ahead of Turner.

Jalen Redmond, undrafted out of the XFL, has an 83.7 PFF grade, fourth among defensive tackles, and a 90.0 run defense grade, first at his position.

Levi Drake Rodriguez, a seventh-round pick, posted a 93.3 overall grade at Lambeau, with eight pressures and two sacks.

This defense is allowing 14 points per game, putting it in company with the 2002 Buccaneers and the 2008 Steelers.

And on Sunday night in Mexico City, these overlooked players get the perfect chance to prove Boone wrong.
