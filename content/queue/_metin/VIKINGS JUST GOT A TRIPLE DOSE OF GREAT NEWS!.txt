Welcome back to Vikings Floor. Let’s get into it.

Derrick Henry.

The Baltimore Ravens running back is the name to watch in this trade discussion. And right away, there’s a distinction that matters.

A move to Minnesota is a rumor and a proposed fit. It is not a confirmed deal.

According to ESPN’s Dan Graziano, Henry was someone to keep half an eye on in potential trade conversations, especially if Baltimore lost in Week 9.

And that loss happened. The Ravens fell to Jacksonville.

That adds another reason to look at the possibility.

But let’s be careful. A conditional report doesn’t prove that Baltimore is shopping Henry, or that the Vikings have made an offer.

Those are different things.

What’s interesting is why Henry would enter this conversation at all, when so much attention has been on Jonathan Taylor.

It’s a potential way to improve the running game without assuming Taylor is the only answer.

And think about the fit. Henry brings a physical, punishing running style that would be very easy for Vikings fans to embrace. He’s a back who wears defenses down in the fourth quarter. A back who turns a 3-yard gain into 7 by refusing to go down. A back whose punishing style breaks opponents physically.

That’s exactly the kind of identity this offense has been searching for.

Still, the excitement has to follow the evidence.

A Ravens loss can change the conversation around their roster. But it doesn’t automatically change their asking price or their willingness to deal.

So this is a situation to monitor, with a clear line between possibility and progress.

Now, Henry’s production gives this idea real substance beyond his reputation.

He has 689 rushing yards and eight touchdowns, according to the discussion around this rumor.

Eight touchdowns.

Let that sink in for a second, because it matters a lot for this Vikings team.

Remember, Minnesota ranks dead last in the NFL in rushing touchdowns. The entire team.

Derrick Henry, by himself, has eight.

That’s a player who still offers meaningful scoring production, even with fair questions about how much of his best football is left.

You don’t have to pretend age doesn’t matter to appreciate what he could bring.

The argument for Henry is that a shorter commitment might deliver immediate help.

The argument against him is that past greatness can’t guarantee the next stretch of production, especially after changing teams midseason.

And the career résumé is substantial.

Henry ranks third all-time in rushing touchdowns and seventh in rushing yards, according to the list cited in this discussion.

Third all-time in rushing touchdowns. That’s legendary company.

That helps explain the attraction. But you wouldn’t make a trade based on all-time rankings alone.

The real question is whether his strengths address Minnesota’s current problem.

So let’s talk about that problem, because it’s real.

According to The Athletic, citing Next Gen Stats, Minnesota ranks 27th in rushing EPA, at minus 28.4.

Compare that with minus 4.7 in 2025.

That’s a huge decline in one year.

EPA, expected points added, measures how much each play changes a team’s expected scoring. So a big negative number means the running game is actively hurting the offense’s chances of scoring.

That’s not just “the run game is a little slow.” That’s “the run game is costing us points.”

And it gives this whole discussion a foundation beyond just wanting a famous name in purple.

We’ve seen the other numbers too. Twenty-second in yards per carry. Dead last in rushing touchdowns. And against Detroit, just 81 rushing yards while the Lions ran for 230.

So yes, the running game needs to improve.

But you still have to be careful about blaming it all on the running backs.

Blocking, execution, and the situations an offense creates all belong in the evaluation.

Remember what we’ve covered. Donovan Jackson, Minnesota’s 2025 first-round pick, is out for the season. Undrafted rookie Joe Huber is starting at left guard. Will Fries has been inconsistent at right guard. And Josh Oliver, the team’s best run-blocking tight end, is out for the year.

Put Derrick Henry behind that blocking, and is he really going to look like Derrick Henry?

Adding another runner only works if the actual problem gets addressed.

So before we go further, here’s my question for you.

Comment “1” if you want Minnesota to go after Derrick Henry as the immediate upgrade.

Comment “2” if you’d keep Aaron Jones and Jordan Mason and focus on improving the support around them.

Either answer makes sense. Let’s see where Vikings Nation lands.

And there’s another layer to this.

Adding Henry would raise questions about roles, and possibly whether Jones or Mason would have to be moved.

To be clear, that’s a possible consequence of the proposal, not a reported decision.

But think about it. Aaron Jones has been playing through a sprained MCL that may date back to training camp, and he’s been a warrior for this team. Jordan Mason just came back from a broken thumb. Both are on contracts that run through this season.

Add Henry, and someone’s workload shrinks dramatically. Maybe someone becomes expendable.

You’d want a real plan for the backfield before celebrating another addition. Because collecting talented players and building a coherent offense are two very different challenges.

Now let’s talk about Jonathan Taylor, because his situation explains exactly why Minnesota needs to keep alternatives open.

According to Jordan Schultz, Indianapolis was waiting until after its Sunday game against Dallas before revisiting trade offers.

The Colts’ position was that they don’t need to move Taylor, and the asking price stayed high.

That gave Indianapolis leverage.

Well, Sunday came and went. The Colts lost to Dallas and fell to 2-7.

So the waiting is over. And Mike Florio believes Indianapolis will take the best offer on the table. But the reported price is still a first-round pick.

And Taylor’s appeal is easy to understand.

He ran for 95 yards and a touchdown against Minnesota in Week 7, against the best scoring defense in football.

And The Athletic lists him with 18 runs of at least 10 yards this season.

Eighteen explosive runs. That’s exactly what Minnesota’s offense is missing. Remember, Kyler Murray, the quarterback, still has the team’s longest run of the season at 35 yards.

But you have to separate wanting that production from accepting whatever price it takes to get it.

Then there’s Tony Pollard.

Pollard belongs in the same evaluation, as an alternative to Henry.

Dan Graziano reported that Pollard was more likely to be traded than either Jonathan Taylor or Saquon Barkley.

That doesn’t make him Minnesota’s next running back. But it makes him a more realistic possibility.

And the interest makes sense. He scored five touchdowns over his previous two games. And The Athletic credits him with 13 runs of at least 10 yards.

He’s also presented as an option on an expiring contract.

And that’s appealing for Minnesota. A shorter commitment could leave room to address the position long-term in the draft.

But even a shorter commitment has to deliver enough improvement to justify the cost.

Then there’s a different kind of option: Tyjae Spears.

The appeal here is speed, receiving ability, and the chance to add another useful back without making a huge investment.

The Athletic’s figures give Spears two runs of at least 10 yards on 18 attempts. Compare that with 13 such runs for Pollard.

But you can’t use those totals as a complete ranking, because opportunity matters. Spears has had far fewer carries. He’s being discussed as a complementary option, and that role should be evaluated differently from the role envisioned for Henry.

Breece Hall’s name is also on that list.

So think about this for a second.

The choice isn’t just about which name gets the loudest reaction.

The real question is what Minnesota actually needs.

A featured back to carry the load? A complementary receiving back out of the backfield? Or better conditions for the runners already here?

Jones and Mason are still part of that calculation.

Henry might offer the most compelling immediate identity. A bruiser. A finisher. A touchdown machine.

Spears could make sense if the goal is depth and versatility.

Pollard sits in the middle, as another potential short-term option who’s been producing right now.

And Taylor is still the biggest prize, if the price ever comes down.

But none of these becomes the right move just because another target looks too expensive or too hard to get.

And patience with the reporting matters too.

A name becoming available, a team expressing interest, and a deal getting done are three separate developments.

We’ve seen that all season. Jonathan Taylor was “not available,” then “Vikings among the favorites,” then “the Giants cooled.” Kirk Cousins was “a possibility,” then “the Raiders aren’t interested.” Jauan Jennings was traded to San Francisco, until he wasn’t.

So until there’s more than a conditional possibility, keep the excitement alive, but keep expectations tied to what’s actually been reported.

Now, here’s the part I promised you at the beginning. And it changes how you should look at every name on this list.

According to Dan Graziano, Minnesota has looked around at running backs. But it doesn’t sound like the Vikings are positioned to take on much salary.

That’s the biggest limitation in this entire conversation.

You can’t responsibly sell Henry as an easy acquisition while leaving that out.

Remember the numbers. Minnesota reportedly has about $4.6 million in available cap space, ranking 26th in the league.

A star running back like Henry would almost certainly need some kind of cap work to fit.

There’s no specific cap solution established here. And you shouldn’t invent one before the football fit can become a realistic transaction.

The financial fit has to work just as much as the football fit.

And Graziano raised another possibility that might be the most important part of this whole discussion.

He said that help for the running game could come through an interior offensive lineman, or a blocking tight end.

And that makes this more interesting than a simple shopping list.

If Minnesota believes Jones and Mason can produce with better blocking in front of them, then improving the line could fix the same problem from another direction.

Think about it. Remember the Johnny Mundt proposal from ESPN’s Ben Solak: a blocking tight end who knows O’Connell’s system, for basically a sixth-round pick. Remember Mo Alie-Cox. Remember Landon Dickerson, the three-time Pro Bowl guard whose run-blocking grade last season would rank first among Minnesota’s current starting linemen.

Those moves cost a lot less than Derrick Henry or Jonathan Taylor. They fit the salary situation. And they might help Jones and Mason just as much as a new running back would help the offense.

So that decision should be judged by its effect on the offense, not by the size of the headline.

Henry is still an intriguing possibility. Third all-time in rushing touchdowns. Eight touchdowns this season. A physical identity that would fit what Minnesota wants to be.

But the salary limitation means the practical alternatives deserve the same serious consideration.

So here’s my conclusion.

Exploring Derrick Henry makes sense. But the price and the fit have to justify it.

And with the deadline arriving at 3 p.m. Central today, Minnesota has just hours to decide which direction to go.

Stretch for Henry’s immediate impact. Pay a first-round pick for Jonathan Taylor. Take the cheaper Tony Pollard route. Add a versatile depth piece like Tyjae Spears. Or protect flexibility and improve the blocking.

Every option has a case. Every option has a cost.
